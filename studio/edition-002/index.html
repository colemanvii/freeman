The Globe

An animated Freeman identity study

THE IDEA

Here’s what I’m imagining. We take the globe mark and let it live on the homepage. The signature stays exactly where it is and doesn’t move. Behind it, the world turns.

It should still look like a flat, printed insignia from another century. It just happens to be rotating, slowly, about once every thirteen seconds.

WHY IT COULD MATTER

Nobody else building restaurants in Georgia has a mark that does this. It’s old-world and very precise at the same time, which is how I think about us.

And it travels. The end card of a film. A jobsite sign. The back of a jacket. The point isn’t animation. It’s identity.

HOW IT’S MADE

This isn’t a video or a spinning picture. The meridians and parallels are projected from a real sphere and drawn, pixel by pixel, onto the same grid as the script. That’s why the lines stay crisp and the edges foreshorten the way a globe should.

A few kilobytes of code, nothing to load. It holds still for anyone whose device asks for reduced motion. The script is the production 160 × 61 bitmap, untouched. The globe’s line count and weight are set for this study and still need matching against the insignia artwork.

FROM EDITION 001  /  REACTION (UNVERIFIED): REFINE

On a Mission

A documentary series about building the company

THE IDEA

We bring in a filmmaker, Tristan, to follow you and the company for a year. About one film a month, ten episodes, on YouTube.

Working title: Freeman — On a Mission. Possibly with Built for Generations underneath it.

The inspiration is how George Heaton documented building Represent: the journey of the company treated as the interesting thing. I want the philosophy, not the look. Our version belongs to hospitality construction and to Cartersville.

WHY IT COULD MATTER

People hire builders they feel they know. Ten honest films would let an owner, an architect or a future hire spend an hour with us before we ever meet.

It shouldn’t be ten advertisements. It should be a record of what building this business was actually like, including the hard parts.

HOW IT LOOKS

Observational, patient, precise. The camera watches the work instead of performing for it.

USE

  • Strong architectural compositions
  • Natural and practical light
  • Unforced portraits
  • Environmental sound
  • Hands, tools, drawings, materials
  • Real conversations
  • Early mornings against finished rooms

AVOID

  • Motivational music
  • Fake candid moments
  • Drone shots for their own sake
  • Corporate interview setups
  • Aggressive transitions
  • Inspirational speeches
  • Staged toughness

WHAT CHANGES IN 002

Refine was the saved reaction, and it came without a note. So what follows is my reading of it, and you should correct it.

Three things change, all of them in chapter 05. The title becomes Built for Generations. The subject moves from the company to the places and the people who make them, with you in the films and not presenting them. And the year starts with one pilot, not a commitment to ten episodes.

    THE IDEA

    Every finished photograph we own gives the credit to someone else. The room belongs to the designer, the plate to the chef, the picture to the photographer. What the builder did is not in the frame.

    The Building Record puts it there. One record per project, made of three things: the drawings, the photographs, and a plain account of what was kept, what was replaced and what was built new.

    WHAT EXISTS TODAY

    More than a sketch. Built Record Study 001 was built on 13 November around Muchacho Decatur: six photographs, five record drawings traced from them, and a short account. The sheet above is that study’s first drawing, running here.

    It lives on a branch, is not linked from any page of freemangc.com, and is marked not to be indexed.

    WHAT IS MISSING

    The real thing. No architect’s drawing of any Freeman project is on file. No dated construction photograph is filed to a project. Scope, dates, floor area and the project team are not written down anywhere I can cite.

    That is why construction is a reserved state on the sheet above, and why the drawings say “traced” on them. The study invents nothing. When the real material arrives, it replaces the tracing.

    Read from Freeman’s published records and credits, checked 26 October 2026. A further set of photographs sits in the Drive folder, including sitework at Barnsley with people in frame; it has not yet been filed to projects.

    How a record gets made

    THE PROTOCOL / FOUR HABITS
    1. AT PRECONSTRUCTIONAsk for the drawings

      Plan, elevations, sections, and written permission to redraw them for publication. Asked once, at the start, as part of how we work.

    2. ON SITEOne position, kept

      One safe, repeatable camera position per room. One person returns to it at each meaningful change, above all before work is closed up.

    3. AT CLOSEOUTWrite it down

      Kept, replaced, new. Dates, area, team. One page, checked by the superintendent who ran the job.

    4. AFTER OPENINGGo back

      The finished photographs from the same positions, then again a year on. The record keeps growing after we leave.

    Commercial rationale

    WHY THIS EARNS ITS COST
    1. WHO IT IS FOR

      The owner, operator or architect choosing a builder for a design-led hospitality project. They are comparing portfolios that all show finished rooms.

    2. WHAT IT WINS

      It is the proposal. For the next pursuit we send the record of the closest job, and it shows how we read drawings and handle an existing building. Designers get their work documented properly, which is a reason to bring us in again.

    3. WHAT IT COSTS

      Mostly habit. A request at the start, a camera position on site, a page at closeout. The assembly time per record is real and still to be measured on the first one.

    4. HOW WE WOULD KNOW

      Records are asked for by name. Partners link to theirs. A pursuit is won where the record was the thing we sent.

      The rules

      SIX, AND THEY DO NOT BEND
      1. Nothing is staged.We may ask someone to wait. We never ask anyone to do it again.
      2. The sound was recorded there.The room, the tools, the weather. Music only if it was playing.
      3. Real speed, hard cuts.A cut lands when the action is finished, not on a beat.
      4. Text states facts.A place, a true date, a name and a trade. One label in, one mark out.
      5. Everyone is credited.Operators, designers, photographers, and each tradesperson who appears, with their consent.
      6. Safety is in the picture.Correct protection in frame, under the superintendent’s authority, always.
