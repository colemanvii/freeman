The account is Built for Generations, and the publication is signed by Freeman.

**5. The 36–48-hour refresh is not in Freeman's record.** The two published refresh projects, The Optimist's Oyster Bar and O-Ku Atlanta, are both described as **three-week** schedules. If Freeman really does restaurant refreshes in 36–48 hours, that is the best documentary premise the company has. Confirm it with real jobs, dates and clients before anything is built on it. If it happened once, it is a film. If it is how Freeman works, it is a series. **Verify.**

**6. The cabin is already a Freeman study.** The 28 September journal and `studio/studies/README.md` describe "Gabe's Cabin / Fay Jones": reference, then sketch, then Gabe's redline, then detail, then construction, then finished result. If that cabin is being designed or built during these five months, it is the spine of the Gabe film: the one building where Gabe is both builder and client. **7. Freeman's identity already exists on the jobsite.** The site's crosshair cursor is the red cross a line laser projects onto raw drywall. The hairlines are snapped chalk lines. The eagle's "impression" motion is a stamp, and so is an inspection tag. The pixel signature is a name written by hand, and carpenters write on studs in pencil. The identity was designed out of the vocabulary of building. The films can find it in the field rather than add it in post, which is the reason graphics can stay rare.

## 1. The thesis

> **Hospitality is the moment a guest walks in. Freeman's work is everything before it.**

A great restaurant or hotel room is designed so that you never think about how it was made. The ease is the product. Freeman's own essay says so: *"Good construction is often invisible in the finished result. Behind every well-considered space are thousands of decisions, most of which no one will ever notice. Those decisions matter."*

So Freeman's publication records what the finished place will hide: the land before the building, the material before the room, the decision before the detail, the night before the opening, the people before the guests. It is filmed and photographed with the restraint the finished room demands.

**Why this is defensible:**

- **It comes from Freeman, not from us.** It restates the Notes essay, the "Carry the Idea Through" sequence (idea → reference → decision → field → resolution → finished place → record) and the line in the studio record: *the idea survives, the building survives, the record survives.*
- **Only a builder can do it.** An architect publishes the drawing, a photographer publishes the finished room, and a restaurant publishes the plate. The hours in between belong to the contractor, and almost nobody records them with attention. FIRMA (Melbourne, founded 2022, "boutique design-led") has built its credibility through architecture press and its relationship with Cera Stribley, who designed its HQ. That is credibility borrowed from the finished image. Freeman's route is the opposite: credibility earned from the making.
- **It proves the business without advertising it.** The hidden work is what a general contractor sells: judgment, trust, sequencing, service, execution. A film about a decision shows judgment. A night-before-opening film shows execution. Nothing needs to be claimed.
- **It travels beyond construction.** Everyone knows the before. A chef's prep hour, a musician's soundcheck, a farmer's morning and a carpenter's layout belong to the same world. That is how a post about a Georgia sawmill, a pastry kitchen at 5 a.m. or a winter field belongs on the account without showing Freeman at work. They are all *before the doors open.*
- **It works on Freeman's timescale.** Built for Generations is a promise about decades. Instagram runs in seconds. The account should look as if it were made by people who think in decades: it returns to the same places, keeps records, and does not chase.

**The test every post must pass:** *Does this show something a finished place depends on but will not show you?* If not, it does not belong.

**Local subject, international standard.** The subject is strictly North Georgia. The standard is set by Wiseman, Reichardt, Ozu, Bêka & Lemoîne and Rural Studio. Hale County, Alabama became internationally significant through William Christenberry's repeated photographs and Rural Studio's buildings. Specificity of place is how the local becomes international, and it is how Freeman balances Southern character with international ambition without saying either.

## 2. One world: the shared art direction

The three treatments look different (section 3 sets out how). They share these rules.

### 2.1 Honesty rules (non-negotiable)

1. **We never stage work.** We may ask someone to wait while we set up. We never ask anyone to repeat an action, and never a hazardous one.
2. **Every sound was recorded where it appears.** No library effects, no foley from elsewhere, no added birds.
3. **Real speed.** No slow motion, no speed ramps, no time-lapse presented as atmosphere.
4. **On-screen text states only facts:** a place, a true date, a true time, a person's name and trade. No invented coordinates, timestamps or "Day 1" counters that aren't real. The website already holds this rule.
5. **Credit everyone.** Every image carries its photographer. Every film credits by name and company each tradesperson who appears, with their consent. No general contractor does this. It turns "respect for the people doing the work" from a phrase on the Company page into a visible practice, and subcontractors will share the work.
6. **Safety is part of the picture.** Correct PPE in frame, always. The crew completes site orientation and works under the superintendent's authority. No lifts, ladders, roofs or drones without approval. A beautiful shot of unsafe work is a liability and a lie about how Freeman runs a site.
7. **No AI-generated or composited construction imagery.** Not in posts, not as B-roll, not in drafts that might leak.

### 2.2 Frame and aspect ratio

The frame should follow the subject, and each treatment has a home frame:

| | Home frame | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 01 Gabe film | **4:3** (1.33) | The shape of a portrait and a doorway. Near-square frames carry *First Cow* and *Perfect Days*. Widescreen says "cinematic"; 4:3 says "attention". It crops cleanly to 4:5 for the feed if shot with a 4:5 safe area. |
| 02 Construction Film | **4:5** (1080 × 1350) | The account's "page". The moving image sits in the same frame as the photographs, so the grid reads as one publication. |
| 03 Publication | **4:5 stills**, composed with a 3:4 safe area for the profile grid preview | One page size, like a magazine. |
| Exceptions | **9:16**, only when the subject is vertical: a stair, a column, a crane pick, a person standing in a doorway | 9:16 should be a choice, not a default. |

No letterbox bars, ever. If a shot needs to be wider, the shot is wrong.

### 2.3 Camera

- **Mostly on sticks.** Handheld only when following a person through a space, held calmly, at walking pace. No gimbal glides, no slider "reveals".
- **The camera stands where a person on site would stand.** Eye height, or the height of the work: a bench, a bar top, a trench. No impossible vantage points. The camera is a guest on site with the same rights as anyone else there.
- **Verticals are vertical.** Architectural discipline: level, true verticals, corrected in post if necessary.
- **Lenses: a small, fixed set.** Two or three primes, roughly 28/35, 50 and 85 full-frame equivalent. No ultra-wides that make rooms lie about their size. No macro sawdust.
- **Hold the frame long enough for something to happen inside it.** Ozu's pillow shots (Noël Burch's term for Ozu's empty corridors and laundry lines between scenes) are our transitions: a room at rest between human scenes.
- **The drone is a drawing instrument.** If it is used at all, it shoots a still, top-down *plan view* of a site, like an architectural drawing. It never makes a reveal or a fly-through. Use one only with Part 107 certification, owner approval and no work happening underneath.

### 2.4 Light and colour

- **Available light.** Work lights are part of the place: sodium, LED panels and halogen stay their own colour. For conversation, at most one bounce or a practical lamp. Nothing that turns a jobsite into a set.
- **Grade towards truth, not a look.** Whites should read as architectural white (the site's paper is `#fff9f1`, warm, not blue). Skin should be true. Red clay should be red. No LUT "film looks", no teal and orange, no crushed blacks, no added grain.
- **Night is allowed to be dark.**

### 2.5 Sound: half of every picture

- **Location sound is the score.** Rooms, birds, footsteps, compressors, saws, rain on a metal roof, the hum of a walk-in cooler, conversation in two languages.
- **Sound leads the cut.** You hear the saw before you see it (J-cuts). Silence, or more truly room tone, is used for real; let two seconds of a room breathe before anyone speaks.
- **No music beds.** If music appears, it was playing in the room. A jobsite radio is copyrighted music, so license it or cut away from it, and never let a platform mute the film. The Gabe film may carry one piece of source music Gabe actually plays, licensed (section 3).
- **Kit:** a 32-bit float recorder, a boom, two wireless lavaliers, a stereo pair for ambiences, and a **contact microphone** to put on a steel bar top or a stud wall while work is done on it. Record a 60-second room tone at every location, every visit. That habit becomes a format (Room Tone, 4.3).
- **Mix for the phone, master for the room.** Check every mix on a phone speaker and earbuds. Low frequencies vanish on phones, so presence has to live in the mids.
- **The muted-autoplay problem.** Most people meet a film with the sound off. So the first second must work as a picture, and speech is subtitled in the house type (2.6). Speech is the one typographic layer allowed to run over footage. Spanish-speaking crews are subtitled in English, and their English-speaking colleagues are subtitled in Spanish where the conversation is bilingual.

### 2.6 Typography and the identity in motion

The identity is American Gothic: Libre Franklin across the site (since 5 November 2026). Paper `#fff9f1`, umber ink `#332e20`, sand `#ddcfb3`, Freeman red `#f93822`. The 160 × 61 pixel signature. The 192 × 60 bitmap Stamp eagle, with its approved reveal, impression and assembly motions.

- **Labels, not titles.** Opening label in Libre Franklin 500, caps, tracked about +.08em, small (about 2% of frame height), lower left, inside safe areas, for no more than three seconds. Grammar copied from the site: `THE OPTIMIST / ATLANTA, GEORGIA` then `TUESDAY 9 FEBRUARY 2027`.
- **End plate:** paper, a beat of stillness, then the Stamp eagle presses in with its impression motion, the same gesture as the FreemanAI film's approval. *The eagle is a stamp: it means "signed off".* No tagline; the handle already says it.
- **The signature is written once a year:** at the end of the Gabe film, and nowhere else in film.
- **Freeman red means a decision.** In The Decision format (4.2), and only there, a still of a drawing or a field photograph may carry one red mark, made with the site's crosshair, at the point where the decision happened.
- **Find the identity in the field before adding it in post:** the red laser cross on drywall, the chalk line, pencil handwriting on a stud, the inspector's stamp, the punch-list tape. These are documentary images that rhyme with the brand. Document them when they happen; never stage them.
- **Graphic posts are rare.** Motion pieces like Material No. 2 work as issue markers, no more than one in ten posts. The films must stand without any graphics. Test every film by removing the label and the end plate; it should still be ours.

### 2.7 Stills and moving images

- **The still is the unit.** Every shoot carries a dedicated stills camera. Frame grabs are a fallback, not a method.
- **The pair is the sentence.** `/photographs` already composes in editorial pairings: landscape / people, forest / Gabe / warm interior, object / conversation, equipment / quiet detail, site / human presence. That is the Freeman still grammar. On Instagram it becomes the carousel: two to four images whose meaning sits between them.
- **A photograph allowed to breathe.** When a carousel holds a video, it should be a 6–10 second locked shot of the same frame as a still, moving: steam off a pour, a worker crossing the doorway, light moving across a floor. Not a cinemagraph (no partial freeze effect). Simply a still frame in real time.

## 3. Treatment 01: Built for Generations

*A 4–6 minute documentary portrait of Gabe Freeman.*

### 3.1 The problem with founder films

The founder film is a fixed form: drone over the land, slow-motion handshake, interview against a dark background with a key light, B-roll of hands on blueprints, swelling strings, a line about family. It makes the subject smaller, because it makes him a spokesman. Gabe should never explain Freeman to the camera. We should watch him *be* it, and let the people and places around him do the explaining.

### 3.2 Premise

**A builder in the one season of the year when he can see the land.** The film is shot across the five months, from bare winter woods in December to new leaves in April. It is cut as one movement through a day, and the seasons visibly change underneath it. Time passing is the subject: Built for Generations is a statement about time, so the film should *show* time rather than say the word.

If the cabin is being built (finding 6), there is a second premise inside the first. For once, Gabe is the client. Every decision he makes for others, he makes for himself: what to keep, what to spend on, what will last. The Fay Jones questions in the study (the sheltering roof, the threshold, cabinetry, the relationship to site) become questions he answers on camera with a pencil, not in an interview.

### 3.3 Structure: four movements, one day, one season turning

**I. Before light (the land).** The cabin, or the land where it will stand, at 5:45 a.m. Total darkness, then a kitchen light. The only sound for twenty seconds is the property: whatever birds are there that morning, wind in winter oaks, the creek if there is one. We record and add nothing. A kettle. A truck door. The first image of Gabe is from behind, in the doorway, looking at the weather. Shot in 4:3 from inside, the doorframe is the picture's frame.

**II. The drive (the voice).** The I-75 corridor between Adairsville and Atlanta is Freeman's geography. Cartersville sits between Barnsley and the city, so the commute *is* the company. We rig the cab with a lav and a dashboard mic, and Cole rides along. Over several drives we record one long, real conversation, never an interview. Its best passages become the film's spine. The picture is calm: the windshield, the road, his hands on the wheel, dawn breaking over the overpass, a gas station at 6:20. Phone calls are heard only on Gabe's side, never the client's.

**III. The work (the people).** We cut out of the truck to a site at 6:30 a.m. Gabe walks into an unfinished room, and the camera stays at the door. We watch him read the room: the slow look up, a hand on a wall, a question to the superintendent. Then three scenes:
- *A decision.* Gabe, a superintendent (Cameron, Mac or Jimmy) and a designer at a mockup or a field condition, all in one take if possible. We hear the reasoning.
- *Respect.* Gabe with a sub's foreman at the tailgate at lunch. The people who work for Freeman talk about him only where they stand, unprompted, or in answer to a single question asked off-camera on a later day. These are not testimonials.
- *The office.* Macee and Dillon. The unseen half of building: schedules, pay applications, the phone. Thirty seconds of the business is not a betrayal of the film. It is the truth that keeps Freeman a company rather than a craft fantasy.

**IV. The room (the reward).** Evening, at a place Freeman built: Barnsley is the natural candidate, the closest finished hospitality work to Cartersville. Service is underway, guests are talking, glasses are moving. Gabe sits at the bar or a table, *unrecognised*. Nobody in the room knows he built it, and the film never tells them. This is the Notes essay made visible: *"The names of the people responsible may be forgotten. The work remains."* Then a cut back to the land at night, to the cabin (or its stakes in the ground), and the signature writes itself on paper. The only time it does so in film all year.

### 3.4 How it achieves intimacy

- **Conversation, not interview.** Gabe talks to Cole, to his superintendents, to the road. Questions are concrete and never abstract: *"What's the first thing you look at when you walk into a room?" "Tell me about a call you got wrong." "Who taught you to read a wall?" "What on this land do you want left alone in fifty years?" "What does a good Tuesday feel like?"* Never: *"What does Built for Generations mean to you?"*
- **Duration.** The audience earns its closeness by staying in long shots with him. Few cuts, and every cut motivated.
- **He is allowed to be uncertain.** If Gabe is not willing to be seen thinking, hesitating or wrong, then make a shorter, humbler film. A portrait without doubt is an advertisement.
- **The family question is his.** Family appears only if Gabe wants it to, and briefly. The film must not use anyone as a symbol.
- **Cole appears once,** across the table with the cabin drawing, because "eye + execution" is the truth of how this company now works, and hiding the author would be a small lie.

### 3.5 Picture, sound, edit

- **4:3, available light, two primes (35 and 50), on sticks** except the walk into the site.
- **Sound:** the cab conversation; ambiences of the land in each season, recorded in the same spot each visit, so the birdsong changes as the months change; room tone at Barnsley during service. **No score.** One exception is allowed: a song Gabe actually plays in the truck, heard from the truck's speakers and licensed. If it cannot be licensed, there is no music.
- **Edit:** hard cuts. The voice lives in the truck and in the rooms, never as narration over montage. The seasonal change is never pointed out; the trees just fill in.
- **Graphics:** a title label at the start (`BUILT FOR GENERATIONS / CARTERSVILLE, GEORGIA`), subtitles where needed, and the signature at the end. Nothing else.
- **Length:** 5:00–5:30 master. A **3:00 Instagram cut** (Reels allows up to three minutes as of this writing; verify at delivery). A **60-second chapter** (Movement I alone works) as the trailer. The master lives on the website and a video host.

**References:** Kelly Reichardt's *Old Joy* and *First Cow* (duration, landscape, a near-square frame, men who don't explain themselves). RaMell Ross's *Hale County This Morning, This Evening* (elliptical Southern intimacy; no exposition). Wim Wenders' *Perfect Days* (the dignity of a working routine). Les Blank (warmth, food, people talking where they live). Anti-references: *Chef's Table* (slow motion, orchestral worship), *Jiro Dreams of Sushi* (score-driven reverence), and any founder film that opens with a drone.

### 3.6 If the cabin is not being built this season

The film keeps its shape. Replace the cabin with the land and with Gabe's relationship to Cartersville: the town, a place he eats, a building downtown he remembers being built or would save. Movement IV still ends in a finished Freeman room. The film is weaker without a building in progress, but only slightly. The land and the anonymous ending carry it.

### 3.7 Premiere

Screen it in a room Freeman built, with the people this account is for: the restaurateurs, designers, architects and developers on the credit list. One evening, one screening, no speeches. The film is the invitation. This is where cultural reach and business meet most directly.

## 4. Treatment 02: The Construction Film

*An original, recurring language for documenting construction.*

### 4.1 Principle: a site is a theatre of decisions with no audience

Construction is full of cinema that nobody records with attention: crews arriving in the dark, a superintendent reading a room, a pour that cannot stop once it starts, a bar top set level by three people holding their breath, the last person turning off the lights. The drama is real. The clock is real. The consequences are real. So we never add drama. We wait for it, frame it, and cut when it is finished.

**The film language, in one sentence:** *Locked frames, real time, location sound, named people, cuts that land when the action completes, and titles that state only the place and the true date.*

**Visual signature (distinct from 01 and 03):** 4:5 frame; daylight and work light; mostly locked-off, wider than expected so the room is in the shot; people small enough to be in context, and close enough to be known.

### 4.2 The formats

Five repeatable formats, each proving something specific about Freeman.

| Format | Length | What it is | Proves |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A TUESDAY** | 60–90 s | One ordinary working day on one site, from gate to lockup, in 12–20 shots. | Execution, order, respect |
| **THE DECISION** | 45–75 s | One decision from problem to resolution: field condition → conversation → mockup → choice → the finished detail. | Judgment |
| **THE REFRESH** *(if verified)* | 3–4 min + 60 s cut | A restaurant closes after last service and reopens on deadline. The real clock is the plot. | Execution under pressure; service to operators |
| **ROOM TONE** | 30 s | The same locked frame and microphone position, recorded before work starts and on opening night. Sound is the story. | Transformation, care |
| **TRADES** | 60 s | One tradesperson, one task, in real time, in their own words, credited by name and company. | Craft; relationships with subs; recruiting |
| *RETURN* (stills) | carousel | Rephotograph finished projects from the identical position, season after season, year after year. | Built for Generations, literally |

**A TUESDAY: the series anchor.** Its structure repeats so the variations become the pleasure, as with Jarmusch's *Paterson*, whose week rhymes day to day:
1. Dark. The gate or the door. A light comes on (sound first).
2. The morning meeting at the tailgate: a wide shot, coffee, the day's sequence.
3. Three or four trades at work. Each gets one locked shot long enough to watch a task begin and end.
4. One small decision, overheard.
5. Lunch. The site is quiet; a pillow shot of the room.
6. Afternoon light moves across the floor.
7. The last person leaves. Lights off. The room at rest, finished one day closer.

Title: `[PLACE] / [CITY, GEORGIA]`, then `TUESDAY [DATE]`. It is always a Tuesday, and always true. If the best day was a Wednesday, the film is called Wednesday. The rule is truth, not the brand.

**THE DECISION: where Freeman red appears.** It opens on a still: the drawing, or a field photograph of the condition, with one red crosshair mark. We follow the conversation. *The designer or owner approves the cut before publication.* Decisions must read as collaboration, never as Freeman fixing someone else's mistake. No GC should be seen to embarrass a designer in public, and the best designers will want to be in these films for that reason.

**THE REFRESH: the film to pitch to restaurateurs.** On-screen text uses true clock times only: `SUNDAY 10:42 PM, LAST TABLE`. Then chairs up, demolition, overnight, a morning crew change, tile and paint curing, final clean, staff arrival, family meal, doors open, the first guest. No music; the clock is the tension. The 60-second cut runs the same arc at the length of a single breath. **This depends on finding 5 being true.** If it is true, it is Freeman's signature film and the first thing to put in front of a hotel developer.

**ROOM TONE: the most original idea here, and the cheapest.** A stereo recording plus one locked frame of a room before work begins: empty, echoing, a compressor ticking somewhere. The same frame and microphone position on opening night: plates, voices, a door, laughter. Fifteen seconds of each, joined with a hard cut. It shows transformation through acoustics rather than spectacle, which almost nobody does. It needs fifteen minutes at the start of each job and fifteen on opening night. **Start recording "befores" on every Freeman job from week one**; the afters will arrive across 2027 and beyond.

**TRADES: the anti-cliché craftsman portrait.** The cliché is backlit sawdust, slow-motion hands and an acoustic guitar. Ours is wide enough to see the person's face and the room, in real time, as they talk about one specific thing: how they learned to set tile on a radius, why they back-butter, what their father did. Credit `[NAME] / [TRADE], [COMPANY]`. Bilingual where it should be.

### 4.3 Rules of the edit

- **Hard cuts only.** No dissolves, no whip pans, no speed ramps.
- **Cut when the action completes, never on a beat.**
- **Sound first.** Every scene change is led by sound.
- **No more than 20 shots per minute.** Most films will use far fewer.
- **Silence is earned:** two seconds of room tone at the head of each film.
- **The film ends on picture.** Then paper, then the eagle stamp.

### 4.4 What makes an ordinary Tuesday worth watching

Three things, and we look for them on every shoot:
1. **A visible competence.** Someone doing something difficult so well it looks easy: three people setting a 14-foot slab level, a superintendent sequencing six trades through one room before 9 a.m.
2. **A small human moment.** A joke at the tailgate, a foreman teaching an apprentice, someone stopping to look at the light.
3. **A before and after inside one day.** The room at 6 a.m. and at 5 p.m. is different, and the film lets us feel the distance.

If a shoot gets none of the three, the film is not made. That is a normal outcome. Plan for about a third of shoots not reaching publication.

**References:** Nikolaus Geyrhalter's *Earth* (*Erde*, 2019), which frames huge earth-moving sites with fixed, patient compositions and lets workers speak. Heinz Emigholz's architecture films (*Sullivan's Banks*, *Schindler's Houses*): static frames and location sound, no narration. Frederick Wiseman's institutional observation (patience, no interviews). Ozu's pillow shots. Bêka & Lemoîne's *Koolhaas Houselife* (a building seen through the person who maintains it). Anti-references: drone reveals, hard-hat POV, time-lapse skylines, "satisfying" loops, trailer-score ticking, the "day in the life" vlog.

## 5. Treatment 03: Freeman / Perspective (the publication)

*The account as an editorial publication from inside a construction company.*

### 5.1 Principle

A magazine has departments, and so does this. Not every post shows Freeman working. Every post is *before the doors open*: the land, materials, people and places that hospitality depends on, made by Freeman's eye and signed by Freeman's grammar.

**Visual signature (distinct from 01 and 02):** stills first, in pairs, at 4:5. The grid is composed in rows of three, the way `/photographs` composes editorial rows. Typographic pages are rare. Moving pieces are short and still.

### 5.2 Departments

| Department | Subject | Example posts | Share of feed |
|---|---|---|---|
| **FIELD** | Construction, still photographs | A pair: chalk line / finished floor. A triptych of the same doorway on three days. | ~35% |
| **FILMS** | Section 4 formats; chapters of 01 | A TUESDAY, ROOM TONE, TRADES | ~20% |
| **PLACE** | Georgia land and towns | The Etowah River in fog near Cartersville; a red-clay cut on a new road; a Main Street facade before its renovation; the same oak each season. | ~15% |
| **PEOPLE** | Restaurateurs, designers, makers, suppliers, crews | A pastry chef at 5 a.m. in a kitchen Freeman built; a millwork shop owner; a quarry foreman; each a portrait plus one exact sentence they said. | ~15% |
| **MATERIAL** | Where things come from | Georgia marble from Tate, Pickens County; a sawmill; a tile maker; reclaimed heart pine. Each with a source, a person and a use. | ~10% |
| **NOTES / MARKS** | Words and identity | One sentence in American Gothic on paper (Ruskin, Gabe, the Notes essay); Material No. 2; an eagle study. | ≤ 5% |

**Restaurants and places that aren't Freeman's.** Only with real relationships and permission, photographed by us, and credited. The goal is the company Freeman keeps, and the people on the credit list are the first subjects. A portrait of a Rocket Farm or Indigo Road chef at prep, in a room Freeman built, says more about Freeman's standing than any project post.

### 5.3 The caption is the second picture

Instagram won't set Libre Franklin, so the identity in captions is grammar, not type:

```
WOODLANDS AT BARNSLEY RESORT / ADAIRSVILLE, GEORGIA
3 FEBRUARY 2027

[One or two plain, exact sentences: what you are looking at and why it matters.]

Interior design: David Thompson Studio. Ownership: South Street Partners.
Photograph: [name].
```

- First line: place, in caps, slash grammar, as on the site. Second line: a true date, only if known.
- Body: written like a museum label by a builder. No adjectives doing the work of evidence: never "stunning", "luxury", "world-class", "craftsmanship". Freeman's own essay is the voice model.
- Credits follow `project-credits.js` roles and spellings. That file is the single source of truth, so add new partners there first.
- No emoji. Hashtags at most two or three that are literally useful (a place, a trade), or none.

### 5.4 Rhythm and grid

- **Two or three feed posts a week**, never more. Stories only when something is actually happening.
- **Compose the grid a row at a time:** one film, one pair, one place or person, so each row of three reads as an editorial spread. Plan in three-week blocks.
- **Never publish anything until three weeks of work are banked** (see 9.4).
- **Highlights are the index:** `FILMS / TRADES / ROOM TONE / PLACES / PEOPLE`. Like the site's navigation, plain and capped.

### 5.5 The moving identity on Instagram

- **The eagle stamp** closes every film (2.6).
- **Issue markers.** Once a month at most, a graphic piece from the existing studies marks a turn: Material No. 2 ("Nothing added, nothing taken away. 2,278."), or Plumb, the weathervane turning into the crosshair, if it survives the "logo spin" test the study itself set.
- **No graphic templates for photographs.** No frames, no logo bug, no title cards over photographs. The photograph and the caption are enough.

**References:** *Apartamento* (lived-in honesty; people in their real places). The Bechers' typologies (a grid of the same object from the same angle: every threshold Freeman built, every bar top edge). William Christenberry's Hale County (the same building photographed for decades). Rural Studio (Southern place, made with care and taken seriously by the world). William Eggleston (Southern colour with no nostalgia). Anti-references: Kinfolk-style styled emptiness, *Garden & Gun* Southern-lifestyle nostalgia, and the architecture-press feed of perfect, uninhabited rooms.

## 6. Recommended direction and why the alternatives are weaker

There were four credible directions for the whole account. Here they are, at full strength, with my reasons.

**A. The Portfolio, Elevated** (where most design-led builders, FIRMA included, sit)
Commissioned photography of finished rooms, material close-ups, polished process reels, architecture press partnerships.
*Weaker because:* the finished room belongs to the architect, the designer and the operator. In that image Freeman is a credit line. Every design-led builder's feed looks alike for this reason. It doesn't use the one thing only Freeman has, which is access to the making. It also depends on budgets for commissioned photography that compete with the films.
*What we keep:* the discipline to pair finished places with evidence, and photography of finished rooms *with* people in them, during service.

**B. The Southern Lifestyle Publication**
Gabe as a character, land, food, music, Americana; something like an in-house *Garden & Gun*.
*Weaker because:* it drifts away from the business. It aestheticizes the South (pedal steel, mason jars, golden hour on a porch). It is exactly the "manufactured appearance of a creative company" the brief warns against. And it competes with media companies that have more money and less reason to be trusted about construction.
*What we keep:* Georgia as subject (PLACE, MATERIAL, PEOPLE), on the condition that each post connects to the before of hospitality.

**C. The Personality Docuseries**
Founder vlogs, site humour, "come with me" reels, fast cuts, trending audio.
*Weaker because:* it works algorithmically and erodes everything the identity has earned. It makes Gabe a performer. It doesn't age, and nobody rewatches a vlog in five years. The people this account is for, the restaurateurs, architects and developers, read it as noise.
*What we keep:* nothing in form. Its one lesson is that people follow people, so the films must name and show the humans.

**D. Before the Doors Open (recommended)**
The observational record of everything before the guest arrives: three treatments, one rule set.
*Stronger because:* it uses Freeman's unique access. It proves judgment and execution without claims. It extends naturally into culture through the before. It matches the restraint of the identity, and it compounds over time (Room Tone afters, Return photographs, the archive).
*Its real risks:* it can turn austere, slow and self-serious, and the algorithm will not reward it quickly. *Mitigations:* keep films short. Insist on people, humour and warmth. Use The Refresh for genuine tension. Hold a hard quality bar, because patience is not the same as length.

## 8. The first three films to shoot

### Film 1: A TUESDAY, at the most active Freeman site in early December

**Why first:** it proves the language, and it builds trust with the superintendents and crews we'll need for everything else. It is the template the series depends on. It's low-risk: one day, no special access beyond a normal site visit.

**Choose the site by:** visible trade work that day (not just inspections), a superintendent willing to be filmed, an owner or operator willing to approve publication, and a room with daylight.

**Plan:**
- *Recce (half day, the week before):* walk the site with the superintendent. Agree safety rules and no-go zones. Pick three or four frames. Record the room tone. Photograph the "before" for Room Tone.
- *Shoot (one long day):* arrive 45 minutes before the crew. Shot list:
  1. Dark gate or door; first headlights (sound first).
  2. Superintendent unlocking; lights on.
  3. Tailgate meeting, wide, at least 90 seconds rolling.
  4–7. Trades: four locked frames, each held through one task's beginning and end.
  8. The overheard decision (be near the superintendent at 9–11 a.m.).
  9. Contact mic on the material being worked.
  10. Lunch: the empty room (pillow shot).
  11. Afternoon light across the floor.
  12. Detail: handwriting on a stud, laser cross, chalk line (only if present).
  13. Last person out; lights off; the room at rest.
  Stills camera throughout. Releases signed the same day, English or Spanish as appropriate.
- *Edit:* five days, then a review with Cole. 75-second target.
- *Approvals:* superintendent (accuracy), Gabe, then the client or operator (design embargo).
- *Deliverables:* the 4:5 film, a 12-frame contact sheet carousel, three to five stills for FIELD, room tone archived.

**Success:** someone with no interest in construction watches to the end. The crew shares it. The client asks for the file.

### Film 2: THE REFRESH (or THE DECISION if no refresh is scheduled)

**Why second:** if the 36–48-hour refresh is real, this is Freeman's most distinctive and commercially powerful film. January is a common slow season for restaurants, so ask every operator client whether a closure is planned between January and March. **Do not shoot until finding 5 is verified and the operator is enthusiastic, not just tolerant.**

**Plan:**
- *Pre-production (two to three weeks before):* the operator's agreement in writing, including when it can be published, normally on reopening. Plan crew shifts with Freeman's schedule. A two-person crew (Tristan plus Cole or an assistant) works in shifts; no one films for 36 hours straight. That is a safety rule too.
- *Coverage:* last table and service ending (operator's staff consent); chairs up; demolition (locked wide, sound); the overnight (work lights, a few faces); dawn shift change; the critical-path trade (tile, paint, millwork set); cure time (a wall drying in a locked frame); the final clean; staff arrival; family meal; door unlocked; first guest (consent at the door, or shot so guests are not identifiable).
- *On-screen text:* true day and time at five to seven points only.
- *Edit:* a 3:30 master (website, YouTube, and Instagram if it can be cut to three minutes) and a 60-second version.
- *Publication:* on or just after the reopening, as a gift to the operator's launch. They share it, and the film reaches their audience.

**Fallback, THE DECISION:** if no refresh happens before March, film one decision on an active job: field condition → conversation → mockup → resolution. It opens on the red-marked still (2.6). The designer approves the cut.

### Film 3: WINTER, the first movement of BUILT FOR GENERATIONS

**Why third, and why it starts now:** the landmark film needs the season to change on screen, so winter has to be captured in December or January or the film loses its structure. The first shoot also starts the relationship. Gabe needs to get used to the camera long before the important scenes.

**Plan:**
- *Before the shoot:* a conversation with Gabe about what the film is and isn't (no testimonial, no hero shots), what he wants kept private, and whether the cabin is in progress (finding 6).
- *Shoot (one day, dawn to dark):* Movement I at the cabin or on the land (dawn, doorway, kettle, truck door); the drive with the cab rigged (record the whole drive); arrival at a site; Gabe reading a room from the doorway; one evening scene if possible. Record the land's ambience at a fixed microphone position, the same spot every visit.
- *Deliverables:* a 45–60 second standalone chapter, *Winter*, which can publish on its own if Gabe approves, plus stills of the land for PLACE and the first hour of cab conversation transcribed.
- *Return visits:* late February (pre-spring) and late March or early April (spring leaf-out; dogwood and redbud season in North Georgia). Shoot the same frame at the land on each visit.

**Running from week one, on every site (not a shoot): ROOM TONE befores.** Fifteen minutes at every job starting during the five months. It is the cheapest long-term asset in the plan.

## 9. The production partnership and the five-month plan

### 9.1 Roles

**Cole: creative director and editor.** Owns the thesis, what we make and why, the grid and the sequence, captions and writing, brand coherence, fact-checking, and final approval of every published piece. Manages access with Gabe and clients.

**Tristan: director, cinematographer, editor and sound.** Owns the craft standard, the production plan (recce, call sheets, kit, safety coordination with superintendents), the edit, and the mix. Has the right to refuse a shot or a cut that breaks the honesty rules. **Credited by name on every film** ("Film: Tristan [surname]"). A serious filmmaker needs authorship, and credit is the same principle we give the trades.

**Gabe: access and accuracy.** Approves anything that touches a client relationship, a business fact or a person on his team. Not an art director.

**Clients and partners** approve anything that shows their space before opening, their people or their designs. Most restaurant concepts are confidential until launch, so expect embargoes and plan publication around openings.

**Two keys:** a piece publishes only when Tristan says it's finished and Cole says it belongs. If they disagree on brand, Cole decides. If they disagree on craft, they reshoot or kill the piece.

|---|---|---|---|

### 9.3 Capacity and output (realistic for one filmmaker plus Cole)

- **Shoot days:** about 30–35 over 22 weeks, roughly 1.5 a week, more in March. Edit days about equal to shoot days.
- **Output by mid-April:**
  - 1 landmark film (5:00 master, 3:00 cut, 60 s trailer)
  - 6–8 Construction Films (2–3 TUESDAYS, 1 REFRESH or DECISION, 2–3 TRADES, the first ROOM TONE if a project opens in the window)
  - 40–50 feed posts in total (two or three a week from mid-January)
  - 2–3 identity pieces
  - About 3,000 catalogued stills and a documented sound library
- **Not in scope:** daily posting, trend reels, paid social, influencer collaborations, a podcast. Each is defensible later, and none belongs in these five months.

### 9.4 Weekly rhythm

- **Monday, 30 minutes, Cole and Tristan (Gabe optional):** what's happening on sites this week, from the superintendents' schedules; what we shoot; what publishes.
- **Moments-on-call list:** superintendents text Cole when one of these is coming: a concrete pour, steel set, a crane pick, a hood or oven fired for the first time, millwork set, sign installation, the owner's walk-through, the night before opening, first service, keys handed over. Tristan comes if he can. Cole carries a stills camera if he can't.
- **Thursday review:** cuts in progress, captions, facts, approvals.
- **Monthly screening:** watch everything published that month in order, as the grid presents it. Does it read as one publication?
- **The bank rule:** never fewer than two weeks of approved posts in reserve. When the bank runs low, publish less; never publish worse.

### 9.5 Archive (private, never in this public repository)

- **Location:** a private Drive or NAS with an offsite copy (three copies, two media, one offsite). Raw camera files are kept; nothing is deleted after an edit.
- **Naming:** `YYYY-MM-DD_PROJECT-SLUG_SHOOT-TYPE`, using the project slugs from `project-credits.js`.
- **Record for every published image or film:** date, place, project, people (with release references), photographer or filmmaker, rights, client approval, embargo date, and the caption source. That record is what lets Fieldwork return to the website honestly.
- **Rights:** confirm who shot each image on `/photographs` and whether it is cleared for social use. The Brand Film 003 notes already had to add a caption credit for Jared Swafford.

### 9.6 Safety and permissions checklist (every shoot)

Site orientation completed; superintendent's authority acknowledged; PPE for crew and in frame; no-go zones agreed; no lifts, ladders, roofs or drones without approval; never ask for an action to be repeated; releases for identifiable people (English or Spanish), and for subcontractor employees, their employer's consent where required; owner or operator permission and embargo terms; no confidential drawings or pricing visible in frame; music in the room noted for licensing or avoided.

## 10. Quality measures

Followers are not the measure in five months. Here is what is.

**Every piece, before it publishes, scored 1–5 by Cole and Tristan:**

| | Question |
|---|---|
| **Truth** | Is every fact sourced, every credit right, and nothing staged? (Must be 5.) |
| **Attention** | Did we see something others would have missed? |
| **Sound** | Does it hold with your eyes closed? |
| **Restraint** | Could any shot, word or graphic be removed? If yes, remove it. |
| **Freeman** | Without the label and the eagle, is it still unmistakably ours? |
| **Business** | Does it show judgment, trust, service, execution or taste, without saying so? |

Publish at 24/30 or above, with Truth at 5. Expect to kill about a third of what is shot.

**Over the five months:**
- **The forward test.** People in the target audience (restaurateurs, designers, architects, developers, hoteliers) send our work to each other. Track it by name, in a simple log of inbound messages and introductions that can be traced to the account.
- **Partners want in.** Operators and designers ask to be filmed, or ask for the file to use at their own opening.
- **Trades share it.** Subcontractors repost the films they are credited in, and the TRADES films bring in hiring enquiries.
- **Saves and shares over likes.** Watch completion rate on the films, not views.
- **Zero failures on things that can't fail:** zero uncredited images, zero unapproved client content, zero safety issues in frame.
- **The archive exists:** 100% of published work has a complete record.
- **The bar:** by April, could Cole show the grid, the landmark film and two Construction Films to a filmmaker, an architect, a restaurateur and a creative director, and have each say "I haven't seen a builder do this"?

**An honest note on the ambition.** "The most compelling GC Instagram account in the world by April 2027" is a good north star and a poor five-month metric. In five months we can make a body of work that deserves that description and a system that keeps producing it. Reputation follows, on the timescale the name promises.

## 11. Risks, open questions and facts to verify

**Verify before anything is published:**
1. **The 36–48-hour refresh.** Which jobs, which clients, what dates? The published refreshes (The Optimist Oyster Bar, O-Ku Atlanta) say three weeks.
2. **The cabin.** Is it existing, in design, or in construction this winter? On whose land, exactly ("outside Cartersville")? What can be shown?
3. **Project naming.** Barnsley Family Pool, Barnsley Gardens or Barnsley Resort? Also confirm the scope and dates for Woodlands at Barnsley Resort and Dugan's Pub (their records have no summaries yet).
4. **Source copy.** Remove "on time and under budget", "ultra high-end", "This project had it all", the "Interior Designer" placeholders, and the repeated "2,000+" and "Complete 2024" unless each is confirmed.
5. **Photograph rights.** Who shot each image in the Drive archive, and is each cleared for Instagram?
6. **The account's current state.** I couldn't see `@builtforgenerations` from this session. Decide whether existing posts stay, are archived, or are kept as "before" before the January relaunch.
7. **Platform specifications:** Reels length, carousel limits and grid preview ratio, checked at delivery. They change.

**Risks:**
- **Austerity.** Restraint becomes dullness when observation is weak. The remedy is people, humour, real tension, and short films, not adding music.
- **Client embargoes** delay construction films until openings. Plan publication around openings; meanwhile publish PLACE, PEOPLE, MATERIAL and FIELD stills that reveal no designs.
- **Access fatigue.** Crews and superintendents tire of cameras. Keep crews small, shoot less often but for longer, and share the films with the crews first.
- **One filmmaker.** Illness or a schedule clash stops the films. The stills discipline (Cole) and the bank rule keep the account alive.
- **Gabe's comfort.** If he does not want an intimate film, make a shorter portrait built on the work and the land, and do not force it.
- **Public repository.** This repo is public. This treatment contains no confidential material, but shot plans, client names before announcement, release forms and footage records must live in the private archive, not here.
